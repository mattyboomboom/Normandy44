# Normandy 1944: content review and plan

September 2026. A review of the 18 moments, the historical threads worth drawing out, and proposed changes. Nothing here has been built yet; each proposal lists the sources behind it and what still needs checking.

## 1. The main thread: Montgomery's hinge at Caen

### The interpretation

The site will tell the campaign as a single plan that worked. On the eastern flank, the British and Canadians fought at Caen to draw in and wear down the German armour. That left the Americans free to take Cherbourg, grind through the bocage and break out in the west.

The evidence for this is strong, and most of it comes from before the event or from the enemy's own dispositions, not from post-war claims.

| Evidence | What it says | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-invasion plan (St Paul's School, April–May 1944) | The campaign would pivot on Caen, with the British and Canadian armies "forming a shoulder to attract and defeat German counter-attacks", while the US armies took the Cotentin and Brittany. It was planned as a ninety-day battle ending on the Seine. | Wikipedia, *Bernard Montgomery* (to trace to Hamilton or Ellis) |
| Montgomery's directive, 30 June 1944 | "My broad policy, once we had secured a firm lodgement area, has always been to draw the main enemy forces in to the battle on our eastern flank, and to fight them there, so that our affairs on the western flank could proceed the easier." | Stacey, *The Canadian Army 1939–1945: An Official Historical Summary* (1948), pp. 194–5 |
| German armour, late June | Second Army faced "eight definitely identified" panzer divisions. | Same, p. 195 |
| Epsom (26 June–1 July) | Forced the Germans to commit II SS Panzer Corps, which had been assembling for a counter-offensive towards Bayeux and the join between the British and American sectors. That counter-offensive was cancelled. | Wikipedia, *Operation Epsom* and its sources |
| German armour on the eve of Cobra (25 July) | Seven armoured divisions faced Second Army (2nd, 21st and 116th Panzer; 1st, 9th, 10th and 12th SS). Two faced the Americans (2nd SS and Panzer Lehr). | Stacey, *The Victory Campaign* (1960), p. 185 |
| The same, in tanks | Around 750 tanks faced the British and Canadians, against about 190 facing First Army. Hastings is no admirer of British performance, which makes the figure more telling. | Hastings, *Overlord*, p. 236, via Wikipedia, *Operation Cobra* |
| Goodwood orders, 15 July | VIII Corps was to "engage the German armour in battle and 'write it down'", with Second Army as a "firm bastion" for the American offensive. | Trew & Badsey (2004) and Ellis (2004 edn), via Wikipedia, *Operation Goodwood* |
| Operation Spring, 25 July | On the day Cobra went in, the Canadians attacked Verrières Ridge to hold the SS panzer divisions in place. It was the Canadian Army's costliest day of the war except Dieppe: about 1,500 casualties, 450 of them killed. | Stacey (1960), p. 193 |
| The price | British infantry casualty rates at times rivalled Passchendaele. Rifle companies were about a fifth of the army and took about 70% of its casualties, and Britain had no reserve of infantry to replace them. That explains the reliance on firepower ("Let metal do it rather than flesh"). | Imperial War Museums, *Tactics and the cost of victory in Normandy* |
| The outcome | The Seine was reached on D+85, against a plan of D+90. | Already on the site |

**Supporting historians:** Terry Copp, *Fields of Fire* (2003); John Buckley, *Monty's Men* (2013); Stephen Hart, *Colossal Cracks* (2000); Nigel Hamilton, *Monty: Master of the Battlefield* (1983).

### How to handle the other view

I'd lead with the hinge interpretation throughout, but I'd acknowledge the criticism once, briefly, in a short "The argument over Caen" section on the About page.

The critics' strongest points are real:

- Caen was a D-Day objective and took 33 days to take.
- For Goodwood, Montgomery's messages to SHAEF sounded like a breakthrough while his written orders set a limited attack. Eisenhower felt misled, and Tedder used it against him (Williams 2004, via Wikipedia).

Carlo D'Este's *Decision in Normandy* (1983) argues that the "master plan" was partly rationalised after the event. Readers who know the subject will expect the site to mention this. Answering it with the German order of battle, which is the strongest card, makes the case more convincing, not less. The answer in one line: whatever Montgomery hoped for on a given day, the panzers went to Caen and stayed there, and that is what made Cobra possible.

### Making the argument visible: a "balance of armour" gauge

This is a new element on the map, a small bar under the day counter. For each moment it shows how many German armoured divisions faced the British and Canadians and how many faced the Americans. It would make the hinge argument without a word of commentary.

- **Data we have:** late June, 8 on the British front (Stacey 1948); 8 July, about 250 tanks facing the Americans (Stacey 1960, p. 165); 25 July, 7 against 2 divisions and about 750 against about 190 tanks (Stacey 1960; Hastings); end of July, 6½ against 1½ (Williams 2004, via Wikipedia).
- **Still to research:** mid-June; early August, when Bluecoat pulled armour west and then Lüttich sent four panzer divisions against the Americans. Candidate sources are Ellis's order-of-battle appendices and the Stacey maps on HyperWar.
- **Rule:** the gauge only shows moments with a sourced count. Other moments show nothing, not an estimate.

## 2. Close-ups of Epsom, Goodwood and Cobra

### What a close-up would show

These three moments would zoom in to about 15–25 km across and play in two or three steps each. Each step would show:

- **Formations:** simplified military unit symbols (infantry cross, armour oval) in national colours, labelled "15th (Scottish)", "11th Armd", "12th SS", "Panzer Lehr" and so on.
- **Lines:** the start line, the front at the end of each step (dated), and objectives drawn dashed.
- **Terrain features:** the ones that decided each battle, such as the Odon crossings, Hill 112, Bourguébus Ridge, the Orne bridges, the Périers–Saint-Lô road and the bombing boxes.
- **Village labels:** added at close-up zoom only.

### Epsom, 26 June–1 July: "The Scottish Corridor"

1. **26 June.** Martlet on the right flank (49th Division at Fontenay and Rauray). 15th Scottish, with 31st Tank Brigade, attack at 07:30 behind a barrage and take Saint-Manvieu and Cheux.
2. **27–28 June.** The Argylls seize the Tourmauville bridge over the Odon. 11th Armoured pushes through the narrow corridor and up Hill 112. Counter-attacks from both flanks come within a kilometre of cutting the corridor.
3. **29 June–1 July.** II SS Panzer Corps (9th and 10th SS) counter-attacks. A captured officer's map gives warning. Dempsey pulls back off Hill 112, and the line holds.

- **Figures:** VIII Corps about 60,000 men. About 4,000 British casualties to 30 June. German tank losses about 125, including Tigers and Panthers. All three are in Wikipedia, *Operation Epsom*, and need checking against Jackson (2006) and Ellis.
- **The point:** the only panzer reserve in the West, meant to strike at the Americans or the join, was used up blocking the British.

### Goodwood, 18–20 July: "The armoured charge"

1. **18 July, 05:45.** About 2,000 heavy and medium bombers (1,056 RAF heavies, 539 Eighth Air Force heavies, 482 Ninth Air Force mediums) hit the corridor east of Caen. 11th Armoured leads over the Orne bridges from the airborne bridgehead at 07:45, with Guards and 7th Armoured behind.
2. **18 July, late morning.** The advance is held at Cagny (a battery of 88 mm guns) and by Kampfgruppe von Luck's assault guns. 1st SS stands on Bourguébus Ridge, and losses mount. Operation Atlantic: the Canadians clear Colombelles and Vaucelles.
3. **19–20 July.** The attack stops short of the ridge in a thunderstorm. The ground won is about 11 km, and southern Caen is finally taken.

- **Figures and the debate over tank losses:** estimates run from 253 (Reynolds) and 314, 130 of them destroyed outright (Trew), to about 400 knocked out, many later recovered (Buckley), and 469, most repairable (Tamelander & Zetterling). The site should show the range and say why the figures differ.
- **The point:** it cost tanks, which the British could replace, not men, which they could not. And it kept 1st SS and 12th SS at Caen the week before Cobra.

### Cobra, 24–28 July: "The breakout"

1. **24–25 July.** A false start on the 24th, when bombs fall short. On the 25th at 09:38, fighter-bombers go in first, then the Eighth Air Force heavies (1,500–1,800) on a box 6,000 by 2,200 yards along the Périers–Saint-Lô road. Short bombing kills 111 Americans, including McNair. 9th, 4th and 30th Infantry break in against what is left of Panzer Lehr (about 2,200 men and 45 armoured vehicles).
2. **26–27 July.** Exploitation: 2nd Armored on the left towards Saint-Gilles and Le Mesnil-Herman, 1st Infantry and 3rd Armored towards Marigny. In the same frame, Operation Spring on Verrières Ridge ties down the SS divisions at Caen.
3. **28–29 July.** VIII Corps rolls down the coast: 4th Armored takes Coutances on the 28th. The Roncey pocket follows: P-47s destroy a trapped German column of 122 tanks and 259 other vehicles (Zaloga 2001, via Wikipedia).

## 3. A new moment: the hedgerow war (7–19 July)

- **Title:** The hedgerow war.
- **Where on the timeline:** between "Half of Caen" (4–9 July) and Goodwood, with 14 July as the headline date.
- **Camera:** close-up on the Saint-Lô sector, from Mont Castre and La Haye-du-Puits in the west to Hill 192 and Saint-Lô.

**Draft text:**
> South of the beaches the country breaks into thousands of small fields, each walled by an earth bank a metre or two high, crowned with hedge and tree roots. Every field is a fortress, every sunken lane an ambush. First Army's July offensive towards Saint-Lô pays about 40,000 casualties for twenty miles.
>
> The answer comes from the ranks. Sergeant Curtis G. Culin of the 102nd Cavalry Reconnaissance Squadron welds steel teeth, cut from German beach obstacles, to the front of a Sherman so it can burst through a hedgerow instead of climbing it and exposing its belly. Bradley watches a demonstration on 14 July; some 500 cutters are made in time for Cobra. Saint-Lô falls on 18–19 July.

**A detail worth getting right:** it wasn't a dozer blade. Culin's device was a set of cutting teeth, soon nicknamed the "Rhino". Dozer tanks existed separately. Hastings records that the idea came from a soldier named Roberts, and that Culin built it. Blumenson thought it made little tactical difference apart from morale. I'd give that one line, because it's the kind of detail that earns a reader's trust.

**Map elements:**
- Mont Castre, where the 90th Division fought.
- Hill 192, where the 2nd Division fought on 11 July.
- Saint-Lô, with Major Howie of the 116th Infantry, killed on 17 July and carried into the town on 19 July.
- La Haye-du-Puits.
- An inset showing a single hedgerow cell: the bank, the hedge, and a Rhino tank cutting through.

**Figures to show:**
- 29th Division: 3,706 casualties.
- 30th Division: 3,934 casualties.
- 35th Division: 2,437 casualties.
- About 500 cutters built.

These come from Wikipedia's *Battle of Saint-Lô* and *Rhino tank* articles. The 40,000 total needs tracing to Blumenson.

## 5. Other improvements found in the review

- **The five unverified figures.** Harrison's *Cross-Channel Attack* is online at HyperWar (`USA-E-XChannel`). It should settle the Cotentin prisoners and the VII Corps casualties, and Stacey or Ellis should settle the German strength in Normandy on D-Day. This is a quick win.
- **British casualties in context.** The end moment could note that British infantry losses at times ran at Passchendaele rates (IWM). That is the human cost of the hinge.
- **Terrain at close-up zoom.** Hand-trace the key roads for the close-ups: Caen–Falaise, Périers–Saint-Lô and Caen–Villers-Bocage. The Odon is already traced. Anything drawn by hand gets marked as approximate on the About page.
- **"Hill 317".** Keep the name, and note in the popover that US maps called it Hill 314. The Sources page already says this.

## 6. What the close-ups need in the code

- **Content:** moments gain optional `steps` (each with its own date, camera, arrows, events, units and lines), plus `units`, `lines` and `zones` for tactical detail. Everything is checked by the schema.
- **Map:** a new tactical layer draws unit symbols, dated lines and zones. The areas of control fade back at close-up zoom so they don't fight the detail.
- **Timeline:** steps appear as smaller ticks grouped under their moment. Playback dwells on each step.
- **Addresses:** `/epsom/`, with steps at `/epsom/2/`.
- **Research:** unit positions come from the official histories' maps (Ellis, Stacey and Blumenson on HyperWar) and are marked approximate. This is the slowest part of the work, about half a day per close-up.

## 7. Suggested order of work

1. The balance-of-armour gauge, the "The argument over Caen" section on About, text edits to existing moments, and the unverified figures. Small, and it establishes the hinge thread straight away.
2. The hedgerow war, as a new ordinary moment with no new map machinery.
3. The tactical layer, with Epsom built first as the pilot. Then Goodwood and Cobra.
4. The plan, as a new moment.
